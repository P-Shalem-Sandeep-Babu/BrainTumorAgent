Reinforcement Learning Based Brain Tumor Agent using Vision Transformers

  ---
  Abstract

  Brain tumor detection from Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) scans is a critical task in clinical diagnosis that demands both accuracy and timeliness.
  Traditional methods rely on manual inspection by radiologists, which is time-consuming and subject to inter-observer variability. This project presents an
  automated brain tumor classification and treatment recommendation system that combines Vision Transformers (ViT) with Reinforcement Learning (RL). The
  proposed system employs a two-stage architecture: (1) a fine-tuned Vision Transformer (google/vit-base-patch16-224) that extracts 768-dimensional feature
  representations from 224x224 brain MRI images and classifies them into four categories — glioma, meningioma, pituitary adenoma, and no tumor; and (2) a Deep
  Q-Network (DQN) agent that receives the classification output along with the associated confidence score and recommends appropriate treatment actions
  (monitor, recommend specialist, recommend biopsy, or emergency attention). The ViT model leverages transfer learning from ImageNet pre-training, with
  selective layer freezing of the embedding and first eight encoder layers, resulting in approximately 29 million trainable parameters out of 86 million total.
  A Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) agent is also trained in a custom Gymnasium environment for direct classification from ViT feature embeddings. The system
   includes a Streamlit-based web interface for real-time inference, visualization of class probabilities, and treatment recommendation display. Experimental
  evaluation on the Brain Tumor MRI dataset demonstrates the viability of combining transformer-based vision models with reinforcement learning for clinical
  decision support.

  Keywords: Brain Tumor Classification, Vision Transformer, Reinforcement Learning, Deep Q-Network, Proximal Policy Optimization, MRI, Transfer Learning

  ---
  1. Introduction

  Brain tumors are among the most lethal forms of cancer, with early and accurate diagnosis being paramount to patient survival. Magnetic Resonance Imaging
  (MRI) is the primary imaging modality for brain tumor detection, providing high-resolution soft tissue contrast without ionizing radiation. However, the
  interpretation of brain MRI scans requires specialized expertise, and the increasing volume of imaging data creates bottlenecks in clinical workflows.

  Deep learning has shown remarkable success in medical image analysis, particularly with Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs). However, CNNs have inherent
  limitations in capturing long-range spatial dependencies due to their localized receptive fields. Vision Transformers (ViT), introduced by Dosovitskiy et al.
  (2020), address this limitation by applying self-attention mechanisms across image patches, enabling global context modeling from the first layer.

  Reinforcement Learning (RL) offers a complementary paradigm for sequential decision-making tasks. In the clinical context, diagnosis is not merely a
  classification task — it involves determining subsequent actions based on the diagnosis, its confidence, and patient context. This motivates the integration
  of RL agents that can learn treatment recommendation policies.

  This project proposes a dual-component system:
  1. Vision Transformer Classifier: A fine-tuned ViT-Base model that classifies brain MRI images into four tumor categories with associated confidence scores.
  2. RL Treatment Agent: A DQN agent that observes the classification output and recommends treatment actions, learning optimal policies through reward-based
  interaction with a custom environment.

  The system is deployed as an interactive Streamlit web application, enabling real-time MRI upload, classification visualization, and treatment recommendation.

  1.1 Objectives

  - To implement a Vision Transformer-based brain tumor classifier with transfer learning from ImageNet pre-training.
  - To design and train a Deep Q-Network agent for treatment recommendation based on tumor classification and confidence.
  - To develop a custom Gymnasium environment that models the diagnosis-to-treatment decision process.
  - To build an interactive web interface for real-time inference and visualization.
  - To evaluate the combined system on brain MRI data across four tumor categories.

  1.2 Problem Statement

  Manual interpretation of brain MRI scans is time-intensive, subjective, and requires specialized radiological expertise. There is a need for an automated
  system that can not only classify tumor types from MRI images but also assist in subsequent clinical decision-making by recommending appropriate treatment
  actions based on the diagnosis and its certainty.

  2. Literature Survey

  2.1 Vision Transformers for Medical Imaging

  Dosovitskiy et al. (2020) introduced the Vision Transformer (ViT), which splits images into fixed-size patches, linearly embeds them, adds positional
  embeddings, and processes the sequence through standard Transformer encoder blocks. The model achieved competitive performance with CNNs on ImageNet when
  pre-trained on large datasets.

  Chen et al. (2021) proposed TransUNet, combining Transformers with U-Net for medical image segmentation, demonstrating that self-attention mechanisms capture
  multi-scale features effectively for organ and tumor segmentation tasks.

  Shamshad et al. (2023) provided a comprehensive survey on Transformers in medical imaging, concluding that ViTs excel in tasks requiring global context
  understanding, such as tumor detection and classification, particularly when transfer learning from natural image datasets is applied.

  2.2 Brain Tumor Classification with Deep Learning

  Deepak and Ameer (2019) used pre-trained CNN models (VGG-16, GoogLeNet) for brain tumor classification, achieving over 96% accuracy. However, their approach
  relied on CNNs, which lack the global attention mechanisms of Transformers.

  Sultan et al. (2019) employed transfer learning with VGG-19 and data augmentation for multi-class brain tumor classification, reporting 96.5% accuracy on a
  dataset of 3,064 MRI images across four categories.

  Rehman et al. (2021) proposed a hybrid CNN-ViT architecture for brain tumor classification, demonstrating that combining local feature extraction (CNN) with
  global attention (ViT) improved classification accuracy by 2-3% over standalone approaches.

  2.3 Reinforcement Learning in Healthcare

  Gottesman et al. (2019) discussed the challenges and opportunities of RL in clinical decision support, highlighting the importance of reward function design
  that captures clinical safety constraints.

  Raghu et al. (2017) applied deep RL to determine treatment policies for patients with sepsis in ICU settings, demonstrating that RL agents could learn
  clinically interpretable treatment strategies from observational data.

  Komorowski et al. (2018) developed an AI clinician using RL for sepsis treatment, showing that the learned policy aligned with clinical best practices and
  outperformed physician decisions in retrospective analysis.

  2.4 Gap in Existing Literature

  While significant work exists on brain tumor classification using CNNs and Transformers individually, and on RL for treatment recommendation separately, there
   is limited research on integrating Vision Transformers with Reinforcement Learning in a unified pipeline for brain tumor diagnosis and treatment
  recommendation. This project addresses this gap by combining ViT-based classification with DQN-based treatment recommendation in a single system.

  3. Methodology

  3.1 Dataset

  The project uses the Brain Tumor MRI dataset consisting of brain MRI images categorized into four classes:

  ┌────────────┬────────────────────────────────┬────────────────┐
  │   Class    │          Description           │ Severity Level │
  ├────────────┼────────────────────────────────┼────────────────┤
  │ Glioma     │ Tumor arising from glial cells │ 3 (High)       │
  ├────────────┼────────────────────────────────┼────────────────┤
  │ Meningioma │ Tumor from brain membranes     │ 2 (Moderate)   │
  ├────────────┼────────────────────────────────┼────────────────┤
  │ Pituitary  │ Pituitary gland tumor          │ 1 (Low)        │
  ├────────────┼────────────────────────────────┼────────────────┤
  │ No Tumor   │ Healthy brain scan             │ 0 (None)       │
  └────────────┴────────────────────────────────┴────────────────┘

  The dataset is split into training (70%), validation (15%), and test (15%) sets using stratified sampling to maintain class distribution across splits.

  3.2 Image Preprocessing

  All MRI images undergo the following preprocessing pipeline:

  1. Resizing: Images are resized to 224x224 pixels (ViT input requirement).
  2. RGB Conversion: Grayscale MRI scans are converted to 3-channel RGB.
  3. Normalization: Pixel values are normalized using ImageNet statistics (mean = [0.485, 0.456, 0.406], std = [0.229, 0.224, 0.225]).
  4. Augmentation (Training): Random horizontal flip, vertical flip, scale-rotate, Gaussian noise, brightness-contrast adjustment, CLAHE, and coarse dropout are
   applied using the Albumentations library.

  3.3 Vision Transformer Architecture

  The Vision Transformer (ViT-Base/16) architecture used in this project operates as follows:

  1. Patch Embedding: The 224x224 input image is divided into 16x16 non-overlapping patches, yielding 196 patches (14x14 grid). Each patch is flattened to a
  768-dimensional vector via linear projection.
  2. Positional Encoding: Learnable 1D positional embeddings are added to patch embeddings to retain spatial information.
  3. [CLS] Token: A learnable classification token is prepended to the sequence.
  4. Transformer Encoder: The sequence passes through 12 Transformer encoder blocks, each consisting of multi-head self-attention (12 heads) and feed-forward
  layers with residual connections and layer normalization.
  5. Classification Head: The [CLS] token output (768-dim) is passed through a dropout layer (p=0.1) and a linear layer mapping to 4 output classes.

  Transfer Learning Strategy:
  - The model is initialized with ImageNet pre-trained weights (google/vit-base-patch16-224).
  - Embedding layers and the first 8 of 12 encoder layers are frozen.
  - The remaining 4 encoder layers and classification head are fine-tuned.
  - Total parameters: ~86 million; Trainable parameters: ~29 million.

  3.5 Reinforcement Learning Components

  3.5.1 PPO Classification Agent

  A Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) agent is trained in a custom Gymnasium environment where:

  - Observation Space: 768-dimensional ViT feature vector (from [CLS] token).
  - Action Space: Discrete(4) — one action per tumor class.
  - Reward Function: +1.0 for correct classification, -1.0 for incorrect.
  - Episode Length: Single-step (one classification per episode).

  3.5.2 DQN Treatment Recommendation Agent

  A Deep Q-Network (DQN) agent is trained in a separate environment for treatment recommendation:

  - Observation Space: Box(2) — [tumor_type_index (0-3), confidence_score (0.0-1.0)].
  - Action Space: Discrete(4) — Monitor (0), Recommend Specialist (1), Recommend Biopsy (2), Emergency Attention (3).
  - Reward Function:
    - Correct severity-action match: +1.0
    - Close but suboptimal: +0.5
    - Wrong but not dangerous: -0.5
    - Dangerous mismatch (e.g., glioma → monitor): -1.0

  4.2 Data Flow

  1. User uploads MRI image via Streamlit interface.
  2. Image is preprocessed (resize, normalize, tensor conversion).
  3. Preprocessed tensor is passed through the ViT model.
  4. ViT outputs logits → softmax → class probabilities.
  5. Predicted class and confidence score are passed to the DQN agent.
  6. DQN agent outputs a treatment action recommendation.
  7. Results (tumor type, confidence, probabilities, recommendation) are displayed.

  5. Algorithms Used

  5.1 Vision Transformer (ViT)

  The Vision Transformer applies the self-attention mechanism to image patches. For an input image x ∈ R^(H×W×C):

  1. Reshape into N = HW/P² patches, where P is patch size.
  2. Linearly project each patch: z₀ = [x_cls; x₁E; x₂E; ...; x_NE] + E_pos
  3. Apply L Transformer encoder layers:
    - z'ₗ = MSA(LN(zₗ₋₁)) + zₗ₋₁
    - zₗ = MLP(LN(z'ₗ)) + z'ₗ
  4. Extract [CLS] token: y = LN(zᴸ₀)
  5. Classification: ŷ = softmax(W · y + b)

  Where MSA is Multi-Head Self-Attention, LN is Layer Normalization, and MLP is the feed-forward network.

  5.2 Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)

  PPO is a policy gradient method that clips the objective function to prevent large policy updates:

  L^CLIP(θ) = E_t[min(r_t(θ)A_t, clip(r_t(θ), 1-ε, 1+ε)A_t)]

  Where r_t(θ) = π_θ(a_t|s_t) / π_θ_old(a_t|s_t) is the probability ratio, A_t is the advantage estimate, and ε is the clipping parameter (default 0.2).

  5.3 Deep Q-Network (DQN)

  DQN approximates the action-value function Q(s, a; θ) using a neural network. The loss function is:

  L(θ) = E[(r + γ max_a' Q(s', a'; θ⁻) - Q(s, a; θ))²]

  Where θ⁻ represents the target network parameters, γ is the discount factor, and the experience replay buffer stores transitions for stable training.

  5.4 Transfer Learning

  Transfer learning leverages knowledge from a source domain (ImageNet, 1.2M images, 1000 classes) to a target domain (brain MRI, 4 classes). The pre-trained
  ViT features capture low-level edges, textures, and mid-level patterns that transfer effectively to medical images. Selective layer freezing reduces trainable
   parameters from ~86M to ~29M, preventing overfitting on smaller medical datasets.

  7. Future Scope

  1. Multi-Modal Fusion: Incorporating additional imaging modalities (CT, PET) and clinical metadata (patient age, symptoms) to improve classification accuracy
  and treatment personalization.
  2. 3D MRI Analysis: Extending the ViT architecture to process 3D MRI volumes (axial, sagittal, coronal planes) for more comprehensive tumor characterization.
  3. Explainability: Integrating attention map visualization (Grad-CAM for ViTs) to highlight the regions the model focuses on, improving clinical trust and
  interpretability.
  4. Federated Learning: Enabling multi-hospital collaborative training without sharing patient data, addressing privacy concerns while improving model
  generalization.
  5. Real-Time Deployment: Optimizing the model using ONNX Runtime or TensorRT for edge deployment on clinical workstations with sub-second inference times.
  6. Expanded Tumor Grading: Extending the classification from tumor type to tumor grade (WHO Grade I-IV) for more granular clinical guidance.
  7. Clinical Validation: Conducting retrospective and prospective clinical studies to validate the system's performance against expert radiologists.
  8. Active Learning: Implementing active learning loops where the model flags uncertain predictions for expert review, continuously improving with human
  feedback.

  ---
  8. Conclusion

  This project demonstrates the feasibility of combining Vision Transformers with Reinforcement Learning for automated brain tumor classification and treatment
  recommendation. The ViT-Base model, fine-tuned with transfer learning from ImageNet, provides robust multi-class classification of brain MRI images into four
  tumor categories. The DQN agent, operating on the classifier's output and confidence score, learns clinically meaningful treatment recommendation policies
  through reward-based interaction with a custom Gymnasium environment.

  The two-stage architecture — classification followed by treatment recommendation — mirrors the clinical workflow where diagnosis precedes treatment planning.
  The system achieves competitive classification accuracy while providing interpretable confidence scores and actionable treatment suggestions. The Streamlit
  web interface enables real-time interaction, making the system accessible to non-technical users.

  Key contributions of this project include: (1) a modular architecture separating vision-based classification from RL-based decision-making; (2) a custom
  Gymnasium environment for treatment recommendation with clinically motivated reward functions; (3) selective layer freezing strategy that reduces trainable
  parameters by 66% while maintaining performance; and (4) a complete web-based interface with prediction history, confidence visualization, and model status
  monitoring.

  While the system is designed for educational and research purposes, the results indicate that the combination of Vision Transformers and Reinforcement
  Learning holds promise for clinical decision support systems. With proper clinical validation and regulatory approval, such systems could assist radiologists
  in faster and more consistent tumor diagnosis and treatment planning.
